### B) SYNTHESIS SCRIPT

If you lived in Perth during the early 1990s, the only way to hear Detroit techno was to find an rare import or stay up well past midnight on a Sunday and hunt for it on your FM dial. You were looking for 92.1, the home of RTRFM. Perth's isolation was total, separated from the rest of Australia by desert and the rest of the world by ocean. Without the internet, the radio was our only lifeline, and RTRFM, known as the sound alternative, was the only place willing to platform the music commercial stations refused to touch.

In those days, being a volunteer presenter meant everything. If you wanted to run a mix show, you brought your own turntables, mixer, and crates of records to the station, built a club setup in the booth, and tore it all down again at six in the morning. Electronic music had to fight for every minute on air. After years of lobbying, the station launched Full Frequency in 1992, the first all-dance-music program on Australian radio.

That institutional legitimacy opened the door for shows like Auditory Spiral, which occupied the deep, isolating hours of Sunday night into Monday morning. For six hours, from midnight until dawn, those airwaves became a zone of absolute creative freedom. Commercial radio called it dead air, but for us, it was the opposite. It was a space to let a 12-minute instrumental track unfold in peace, without the constraints of daytime formatting.

The show was architected by DJ Shamus and Nicolai. Shamus built the signature deep and dark aesthetic, creating hypnotic, continuous journeys through minimal and Detroit techno. Nicolai brought a wider, more experimental sensibility to the booth. For ravers, shift workers, and insomniacs scattered across the suburbs, that broadcast was an invisible gathering point, a localized electronic hearth. We were all connected by those FM radio waves, waiting for the next track in a scene built on friendships, warehouse parties, and the shared discovery of sounds that, for a few hours every week, felt like they belonged only to us.

You can read more about the history of Perth's electronic underground and see the archive of our broadcasts at adrianwedd.com.
