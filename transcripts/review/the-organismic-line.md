### B) SYNTHESIS SCRIPT

The previous post in this series argued that human prediction is metabolically grounded while AI prediction is not, and that blurring these two concepts obscures a critical distinction. It named the problem. Today, we’re naming the thinkers who are blurring those lines.

Four figures stand out. Each tries to push the predictive processing or active inference framework into the realm of artificial intelligence, and each effort hits a different wall.

Karl Friston is the most prominent. His Free Energy Principle is designed for substrate generality. He argues that self-organizing systems, whether biological or artificial, can be described using Markov blankets to separate internal, sensory, and active states. In his view, active inference is a universal imperative for any system that persists over time. For Friston, the closer AI gets to these neuronal dynamics, the closer we get to true brain-like computing. The slogan is simple: model evidence is all you need. The tension here is that Friston’s formalism is so abstract it can treat an organism that dies from a failed prediction and an AI agent that outputs a slightly higher loss number as mathematically identical. But the grammar of the math isn't the same as the stakes of life.

Geoffrey Hinton takes a different route, focusing on the hardware. He distinguishes between what he calls mortal computation—where knowledge is inseparable from the physical brain—and the immortal nature of digital weights that can be copied and moved. Hinton frames this as an engineering trade-off: digital systems gain immortality and portability, while biological, analogue systems trade that away for extreme energy efficiency. But from an organismic perspective, mortality isn’t just a disadvantage to be engineered around; it is the very condition that gives a system’s predictions any real meaning. An organism predicts because being wrong is fatal, not because being wrong is inefficient.

The deeper issue is that if you define sentience purely through formal models, the organismic objection looks like a category error. It demands metabolism, but the formal definition never asked for it. If you define sentience biologically, then these formal models have simply explained away the real phenomenon.

Ultimately, this is a fork in the road. Which direction you take determines what you actually mean when you call a machine sentient.
