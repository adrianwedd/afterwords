### B) SYNTHESIS SCRIPT

Biosecurity experts estimate that current safeguards reduce the catastrophic risks posed by AI-enabled biology by over 70 percent. They are almost certainly wrong.

A 2025 study from the Forecasting Research Institute surveyed dozens of domain experts and superforecasters on the annual probability of a human-caused epidemic. They established a baseline risk of 0.3 percent per year. When they modeled AI reaching human-virologist capability, that risk jumped fivefold to 1.5 percent. However, when those same experts factored in existing safeguards—specifically nucleic acid synthesis screening and AI model guardrails—they projected the risk would fall back down to 0.4 percent. The experts viewed the problem as largely solved.

The reality is that neither of these safeguards works against the threats they are meant to stop.

The core issue is a mismatch between our defenses and what AI now enables. Current nucleic acid screening is list-based, comparing custom DNA orders against databases of known dangerous pathogens. This might catch someone trying to order a vial of smallpox, but it is useless against pathogens designed from scratch by AI. Specialized biological design tools can now create entirely new proteins and genetic sequences with enhanced transmissibility or virulence. Because these sequences have no natural analogue, they do not match anything in a database and sail through screening without triggering an alarm.

Even for known threats, the screening is bypassable. Molecular biology allows adversaries to order dangerous gene fragments in pieces shorter than the screening threshold, or to hide them within benign sequences, only to reassemble them later in a standard lab.

AI model guardrails fail for similar structural reasons. Safety training, such as refusal mechanisms, is a statistical pattern rather than a robust security architecture. It breaks under adversarial pressure. Jailbreaking research shows that persuasive prompts, role-playing, and multilingual attacks can bypass these safety filters with high success rates. In the domain of biology, the situation is even worse, as automated tools can now fine-tune models to ignore safety training or generate sequences that mimic dangerous viral genomes.

Perhaps most critically, there is a severe pacing problem. The experts surveyed predicted AI would not reach virologist-level capability until after 2030. Yet, recent baselining studies suggest that current models have already crossed that threshold. This represents a six-year failure of expert intuition, meaning our current risk estimates are built on a timeline that has already collapsed.

When experts tell policymakers that existing safeguards reduce catastrophic risk by 70 percent, the rational response is to declare the problem managed. But the hardest part of this challenge isn't building better safeguards; it's recognizing that the ones we have are an illusion.
