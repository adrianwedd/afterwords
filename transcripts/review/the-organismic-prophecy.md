### B) SYNTHESIS SCRIPT

The old mid-twentieth-century idea of the brain as a passive input-output processor is effectively dead in neuroscience. What we have now is something much more interesting. Your brain is a prediction engine that runs ahead of the world, using sensory data only to correct itself. It doesn't just perceive; it guesses the world into place. 

Predicting is expensive. Your brain is only two percent of your body mass, but it consumes twenty percent of your daily metabolic budget. Every guess your brain makes is paid for in glucose. So, it's more accurate to say your brain isn't just predicting; it’s running an energy budget against an indifferent universe, where prediction is simply the cheapest survival strategy it has.

The problem arises when we apply terms like prediction, error, hallucination, and attention to artificial intelligence. We share the same vocabulary, but the words don't mean the same thing. 

There is a crucial distinction between predictive coding and active inference. Predictive coding is a mechanism, a way of organizing data that can be implemented in silicon. Modern language models use structures that are remarkably analogous. But active inference is a framework specifically for living systems. It’s not just about minimizing prediction error; it’s about an organism acting on the world to keep itself alive. It is defined by metabolism and embodiment. Brains and language models might share a mechanism, but they do not share a framework.

This is where allostasis comes in. While classical physiology focused on returning to a setpoint, allostasis is about pre-positioning resources before a deficit even occurs. Your heart rate climbs before you stand; your cortisol rises before the alarm sounds. It’s an audit of metabolic spend against anticipated demand. When that audit fails, the system can no longer predict its own needs, which is the physical substrate for things like chronic burnout.

We often hear that perception is just controlled hallucination. While that's a popular line, it's incomplete. If perception were purely internal, the tools we use to study it would be hallucinated too. The truth is that perception isn't something that happens just inside your skull. It’s a relationship between you and your environment. Your body, not your skull, is the boundary of your experience.
