### B) SYNTHESIS SCRIPT

You are spending your time demonstrating how AI systems can fail in dangerous ways, and the technical work is hard but achievable. The part that keeps you up at night is what happens after you prove something is broken, because far too often, the answer is nothing. This is not just an AI issue. It is a recurring pattern in large organizations where the gap between knowing a problem exists and doing something about it is not an information problem. It is a structural one.

There is a flawed assumption that if you just find a problem and present the evidence, decision-makers will act. In reality, before anyone acts on evidence, they evaluate what accepting it will cost them personally and institutionally. Truth creates local costs, like blame for past decisions, budget for rework, or public embarrassment. When those costs are not managed, inaction becomes the locally rational choice, even if it leads to systemic failure.

I have identified four structural mechanisms that keep demonstrated risks buried. First, responsibility without authority. The people who find risks, like security researchers or quality engineers, are rarely the ones who can allocate the time or budget to fix them. Findings become tickets that sit in an archive. Second, risk discovery can feel like a career threat. If surfacing a problem is treated as creating exposure rather than providing value, people stop reporting truths that are unsafe to speak. Third, compliance often substitutes for reality. Organizations can have perfect risk registers and documented controls that are never actually tested against real failure scenarios, making their safety symbolic. Finally, diffuse accountability ensures that risk lives in the gaps between departments, where everyone can see the problem, but no one is required to own the solution.

Demonstrating a risk does not always create urgency. Often, it forces the organization to defend its own identity, making the vulnerability a political liability rather than a technical priority. Fixing this requires removing the blame, funding the necessary rework, and creating safe channels for escalation. You can learn more about how organizations can break this cycle at my website.
