### B) SYNTHESIS SCRIPT

What if William Gibson’s Neuromancer wasn’t actually about drug addiction? What if it was an accidental, perfectly accurate map of the ADHD experience, written more than a decade before we had the language to describe it?

In 1984, the DSM-III categorized attention deficit disorder as a childhood issue, with adult persistence relegated only to a residual type. Neurodiversity as a concept didn’t exist. And yet, that same year, Gibson published a novel that encodes, in extraordinary detail, what it feels like when your cognition depends on an interface that isn’t there.

My essay, Before the Words Existed, is a scholarly close reading of Neuromancer through the lens of cognitive mismatch. I argue that Case’s relationship with cyberspace isn’t a metaphor for drugs or noir alienation. It is a reversible, interface-dependent reconfiguration of attention, memory, and agency that maps precisely onto patterns that wouldn’t be clinically named for years.

I track this through three specific areas. First, reversibility: Case’s competence returns immediately when he accesses the matrix—not through healing, but through environmental fit. Second, precision: his memories sharpen specifically within procedural domains, which suggests that his disruption is domain-specific rather than a generalized failure of intelligence. And third, relationality: the dysfunction originates in the mismatch between his cognitive style and the available affordances, rather than being an inherent defect in the person.

This isn’t just a retrospective projection. The thesis demonstrates that Gibson’s language preserves an experiential structure that the era could feel but could not yet articulate. The novel circulated that structure across our culture a full decade before the vocabulary solidified.

The analysis also looks at Molly Millions as a cognitive contrast—her sensorium is optimized for embodied precision, whereas Case’s is built for abstract pattern-recognition—and contrasts the temporal signatures of chemical addiction against this specific kind of access-dependency. I also trace the same pre-clinical sensor patterns in the work of Philip K. Dick, showing how speculative fiction often grants authors permission to describe what institutional language cannot yet contain.
