### B) SYNTHESIS SCRIPT

You are looking at a classic organizational trap. Most companies are currently stuck in the trough of the AI productivity J-curve. They are buying models and running pilots, but the expected productivity dividend just isn't showing up. This isn't a failure of the technology. It is a failure of investment. Transformative tech only becomes productive when you build the intangible capital around it, like redesigned processes, real data governance, and new accountability structures. Until that work is done, productivity usually drops.

The exact same thing is happening to AI safety. Right now, most safety budgets aren't being treated as capital formation. They are treated like pilot projects. They are funded from discretionary budgets, evaluated on a quarterly basis, and expected to produce reassurance just fast enough to keep deployment moving. But safety isn't a moral category first. It is an economic one. If organizations refuse to internalize the cost of building safe systems during that J-curve trough, they are just manufacturing risk-blindness. They are treating safety as a cost center to be minimized, rather than an essential asset that makes the technology work.

The core of the problem is that organizations are splitting transformation and safety into two different worlds. Transformation gets the strategic budget and is measured on velocity and business value. Safety gets a smaller, defensive budget and is measured on whether it produces enough confidence for the transformation to proceed. This forces safety to be cheap, legible, and quick.

This is why behavioral safety—things like model training, adversarial evaluations, and model cards—gets all the funding. It is research-oriented, it lives inside the model, and it produces reports that executives and regulators can easily understand. It keeps safety as a procurement property. But architectural safety—like runtime monitors, isolation boundaries, policy gates, and real incident response—is expensive. It requires engineering, governance, and the authority to actually block a deployment. Because it is expensive and slow, it gets deferred.

The result is that companies are building inadequate safety that just looks complete on paper. They want the assurance of alignment without paying for the architecture of control. Meanwhile, their systems are becoming more agentic, more tool-using, and increasingly impossible to bound after the fact. The economics currently drive organizations toward the safety work that is cheapest to buy, rather than the safety work that actually constrains failure.

If you want to see how these dynamics play out in your own organization, read the full post at adrianwedd.com/blog/economics-of-inadequate-safety.
