### B) SYNTHESIS SCRIPT

You’re looking at a fascinating disconnect in modern economics. Four major forecasters—Goldman Sachs, MIT’s Daron Acemoglu, PwC, and McKinsey—have published wildly different projections for AI’s economic impact. At first glance, you might think these numbers are just competing guesses about the same outcome. But when you look closer, you realize they aren't actually measuring the same thing. The divergence isn't a failure of analysis; the divergence is the analysis. It tells you exactly where the uncertainty in our future lives.

The optimistic models, like those from Goldman Sachs, PwC, and McKinsey, treat AI as a general-purpose technology, much like electricity or the internet. They look at historical precedents where new infrastructure compounded productivity over decades. They model how AI automates a huge range of tasks, lowering costs and driving massive output gains.

Then you have the more conservative perspective, like Daron Acemoglu’s. His work doesn't deny that AI is powerful, but it challenges the assumption that we can easily translate technical capabilities into economy-wide automation. He points out that only a small slice of current tasks are actually cost-effectively automatable today. When you factor in the real-world costs of deployment, organizational inertia, and the sheer difficulty of integrating new systems, the projected impact shrinks significantly.

So, where do these experts actually agree? They all recognize that these gains will be uneven. AI doesn't lift all boats equally; it concentrates productivity in specific sectors and favors those who can complement these new tools. They also agree on the existence of a lag. We’ve seen this before with the "productivity paradox," where technology becomes ubiquitous long before it actually shows up in GDP statistics. Finally, everyone agrees that the critical variable isn't just the math of the models—it’s the pace of adoption. It’s a sociological and organizational question as much as a technical one.

Ultimately, the uncertainty you see in these forecasts is the correct output for this moment. We are in the early stages where the technology is demonstrably capable, yet the economy-wide effects are still being negotiated. The practical takeaway for you is to act on what is certain—the changing cost structures of decision-making and information retrieval—while hedging your strategies against the wide range of potential outcomes. We are living through the integration phase, and that transition is rarely as fast as the hype suggests, but rarely as insignificant as the skeptics fear.
