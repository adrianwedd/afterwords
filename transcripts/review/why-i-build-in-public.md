### B) SYNTHESIS SCRIPT

Every project you see on this site includes a link to its source code. Most of those links point to repositories that are intentionally messy, full of commit messages like fix the thing, or abandoned branches named experiment three. That is a deliberate choice. 

The default posture for most technical work is polished perfection. You usually only see the finished product, and you read the documentation after the dust has settled. This presentation often suggests that a system was designed top-down from the start, when in reality, the creator probably stumbled into it sideways after several false starts and a dependency that turned out to be unmaintained.

I have spent years presenting my work that way, and it is exhausting. More importantly, it is dishonest. Polish is craft, but the highly curated version of a project teaches nobody anything. A reader might learn what you built, but they never learn how you built it—which is the part that would actually help them.

Building in public, for me, means something specific. It means the commit log is the real changelog, showing the actual sequence of decisions, mistakes, and reversals. It means issues remain open as a space to think out loud, often capturing me arguing with myself about what to build next. It means the colophon lists every part of the stack and the design decisions, so if you want to build something similar, you can see exactly how it works. And it means shipping drafts. Not every project here is finished; some are experiments that went nowhere. I leave them listed because those dead ends are an essential part of the map.

Building in public has a real cost. You expose your bad decisions before you have had time to fix them. Someone might look at your code on the worst day of a refactor and form an opinion about your skills. You cannot control that narrative. But the truth is, the smooth, perfect narrative was always a fiction. The only question is whether you spend your energy maintaining that fiction, or if you redirect that energy into the work itself.

I do not do this for an audience. I do it for the version of me from six months ago—the one who was trying to figure something out and could not find a single honest example of someone else doing it badly first, and then doing it well. If you are building something and it feels messy, show it anyway. The mess is the most useful part of the process. You can see how I work at github.com/adrianwedd.
